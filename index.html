02 / Human Need

Awareness × Expression

Not every health need that exists becomes an expressed demand. The distinction between latent and apparent needs helps identify where behavioral intervention is required.

Latent
Apparent / Expressed
Unaware
Unaware + Latent

The person does not recognize the problem or need.

Unaware + Apparent

The problem is observable, but its meaning or cause is not recognized.

Aware
Aware + Latent

The person knows about the issue but has not activated behavior.

Aware + Apparent

The person recognizes the problem and actively expresses a need or intent.

03 / Theory Stack

Major Health Behavior Theories

Each theory explains a different part of the behavior system. They are complementary rather than interchangeable.

Risk perception

Health Belief Model

Explains how perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers and cues to action influence health behavior.

Perceived threat + Benefits − Barriers + Cue → Action
Intention

Theory of Planned Behavior

Behavior is influenced by attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control through behavioral intention.

Attitude + Norms + Control → Intention → Behavior
Behavior system

COM-B

Behavior occurs when capability, opportunity and motivation come together in the right context.

Capability + Opportunity + Motivation → Behavior
Learning

Social Cognitive Theory

Behavior emerges from interactions between personal factors, behavior and environment. Self-efficacy is central.

Person ↔ Behavior ↔ Environment
Autonomy

Self-Determination Theory

Sustained motivation is supported by autonomy, competence and relatedness.

Autonomy + Competence + Relatedness → Internal Motivation
Readiness

Transtheoretical Model

People may move through stages of precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance.

Readiness → Appropriate Intervention
Action design

Fogg Behavior Model

Behavior becomes more likely when motivation, ability and a prompt converge at the same moment.

Motivation × Ability × Prompt → Behavior
Habit

Habit Loop

Repeated behavior can become increasingly automatic when a cue reliably leads to a routine and reinforcing consequence.

Cue → Routine → Reward → Repetition
Behavior economics

Choice Architecture

The structure of environments can influence behavior by changing defaults, friction, salience and convenience.

Environment → Choice → Behavior
04 / Behavioral Diagnosis

COM-B Diagnostic Model

When a desired health behavior is not happening, diagnose the missing component before designing an intervention.

C

Capability

Does the person have the knowledge, skills, physical capacity and psychological ability to perform the behavior?

Intervention examples:

Education · Training · Skill building · Planning

O

Opportunity

Does the physical and social environment make the behavior possible, accessible and socially supported?

Intervention examples:

Access · Convenience · Infrastructure · Social support

M

Motivation

Does the person have sufficient reflective and automatic motivation to perform the behavior?

Intervention examples:

Goals · Feedback · Rewards · Identity · Emotional relevance

07 / Design Principles

What Health Behavior Design Should Optimize

Reduce friction Make the desired action easier than the competing behavior.
Create immediate value Don't rely only on distant health benefits.
Build self-efficacy Create small achievable successes.
Design the environment Change context instead of relying entirely on willpower.
Respect autonomy Support informed choices rather than coercion.
Match readiness Different stages require different interventions.
Use feedback Make progress visible and understandable.
Design for maintenance The real goal is sustained behavior, not a single action.
